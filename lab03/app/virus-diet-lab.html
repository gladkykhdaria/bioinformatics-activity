Reality check. This is a classroom thought experiment, not a treatment.
  • A virus doesn't eat your food. It builds its proteins inside your cells, from the same amino acid supply your own cells use.
  • Your body keeps blood amino acid levels steady and takes them from muscle when food is short, so a diet barely changes what a cell has.
  • Cutting an amino acid starves your own cells first, immune cells included.
  • No diet like this has good evidence against viral infections. The best-known try (low arginine, high lysine for cold sores) has weak, mixed results.
  • Don't fast while sick without a doctor's advice. And the sample virus infects plants, not people.

How it works, in simple words

  1. Read the genome

    A FASTA file is a name line starting with >, then DNA letters split over many lines. The app glues the letters into one string and unzips .gz files by itself.

    CS: parse a text file into a string

  2. Count codons

    A 3-letter window starts at the first letter, reads the codon, adds 1 to its counter, then moves one letter to the right. A genome of N letters gives N − 2 windows.

    CS: sliding window + dictionary of 64 counters

  3. Find the genes

    A gene starts at ATG and ends at TAA, TAG or TGA. The app tries all 6 reading frames (3 on each strand), because viruses keep genes on both strands. Very short stretches are usually random, so they're dropped.

    CS: scan in steps of 3, six times

  4. Translate and count amino acids

    Each codon in a gene is looked up in the genetic code table, and the 20 amino acids are counted.

    CS: lookup table + counter

  5. Compare with a normal protein

    Average proteins (UniProt/Swiss-Prot) are about 9.7% leucine, 1.1% tryptophan, and so on. Virus share ÷ average share gives a ratio: ×2 means twice as much as normal.

    CS: observed / expected, then sort

  6. Turn it into food

    Your body makes 11 of the 20 amino acids itself, so only the ones that come from food count. The app takes the top 4 overused ones and lists foods rich in them (limit) and foods low in them (prefer).

    CS: filter, then map to a food table

Glossary

Genome
All the DNA of an organism. Think of it as the hard drive.
Gene
One stretch of DNA that codes for one protein. One file on the drive.
Codon
3 letters of DNA or RNA that stand for one amino acid. One instruction.
Reading frame
Where you start cutting the DNA into 3-letter pieces. Starting 1 letter later gives completely different codons.
ORF
Open reading frame: from a start codon to the next stop codon with no stop in between. A gene candidate.
GC content
The share of G and C letters. Low GC means A/T-rich codons dominate.
Essential amino acid
One your body can't make, so it has to come from food: His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val.
Codon usage bias
Organisms prefer some codons over others for the same amino acid. It works like a fingerprint.
FASTA
A simple text format for sequences: a > name line, then the letters.
Average protein composition: UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot. Food lists are general sources, not measured values.
